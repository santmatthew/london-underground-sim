# Accurate Tube station models exist, locked away

Nobody publishes an accurate, downloadable 3D model of a whole London Underground station. Survey-grade geometry certainly exists. Victoria, Bond Street, Bank, Tottenham Court Road, Euston and every Elizabeth line interchange were laser-scanned and modelled in BIM during their upgrades. All of that sits in TfL and contractor systems, and TfL routinely refuses FOI requests for drawings under s24 (national security) and s38 (health and safety). The open 3D corpus amounts to **11 schematic Blender models from 2012** (andrewgodwin/stations, non-commercial licence), built "from memory" and on foot. Everything else in the open is either 2.5D or numeric. That includes OpenStreetMap indoor polygons, which TfL paid its contractor MENTZ to add for roughly 30 major stations, and TfL's GTFS pathways and levels topology. It also includes three FOI releases: per-platform depth figures, platform lengths, and about 120 not-to-scale axonometric diagrams, which remain TfL copyright. Games such as Train Sim World's Bakerloo line model platforms convincingly, but their EULAs bar asset extraction and nothing confirms they model full street-to-platform interiors. The realistic route to a scaled whole-station model is fusion. Take OSM plan polygons, anchor them vertically with FOI depth data, use escalator geometry and the axonometrics only for topology, and validate on site. Expect about ±1–2 m vertical error and metre-to-tens-of-metres horizontal error. Plan on tens of hours per complex interchange.

The best open inputs are numbers and 2D polygons, not meshes. The only open meshes are too schematic to trust for dimensions. And the accurate data cannot be obtained by any public route.

The ranking rewards one property above all: whether the data constrains real metres. OSM ranks first despite its patchiness because it is the only open source with true plan geometry. The depth spreadsheet ranks second because z is the dimension every other source gets wrong.

## Survey-grade geometry sits behind s24 and s38

The accurate models exist, and on a large scale. At Victoria, the BAM Nuttall–Taylor Woodrow joint venture built a BIM model that incorporated laser surveys and roughly 2,500 jet-grout columns. Its stated aim was to give "London Underground with an accurate record of assets" for the station's whole life ([Construction Management](https://constructionmanagement.co.uk/victoria-sta8tion-u6pgrade-exem8plar-bim/)). Plowman Craven's high-definition scans of the station interior fed it ([Plowman Craven](https://www.plowmancraven.co.uk/uploads/documents/case-studies/victoria-station-upgrade.pdf)). At Bond Street, Costain–Laing O'Rourke scanned the existing escalator barrel to clash-check the new, larger escalators ([Costain](https://www.costain.com/news/news-releases/innovation-at-bond-street/)). Bridgeway took **about 1,900 colour scans over 4 months** at Euston, including the LU concourses, and the data went into the client's Common Data Environment ([Rail Engineer](https://www.railengineer.co.uk/digitising-euston/)). Crossrail alone produced **1.275 million CAD model files and 650,000 asset tags** in Bentley ProjectWise, eB and AssetWise ([Crossrail BIM presentation](https://www.htwg-konstanz.de/fileadmin/pub/konferenzen/applied5d/Crossrail.pdf)). The Learning Legacy publishes process documents about that BIM, not the models themselves ([Learning Legacy](https://learninglegacy.crossrail.co.uk/learning-legacy-themes/information-management-and-technology/asset-information/)). In 2014 an LU surveyor described terrestrial laser scanning as "the default way of surveying" complex stations ([Geo Week News](https://www.geoweeknews.com/news/thats-the-point-finding-space-under-london)).

None of this has leaked into the public domain, and the FOI record explains why. From 2015 to 2024 the pattern holds. Schematic axonometrics are released, sometimes with redactions. Scaled drawings are refused. TfL withheld Woolwich's technical drawings in full under s24(1) and s38 because they could "assist persons wishing to disrupt London's transport infrastructure" ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/drawings_of_woolwich_station)). It refused even the axonometrics for Elizabeth line-only stations, citing the DfT National Railway Security Programme ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/updated_axonometric_drawings_of)). The research found **no FOI request for BIM, CAD or point clouds of any station**. Given these precedents, such a request would almost certainly be refused, and s43 (contractor commercial interest) or s12 (cost) could be added. One crack exists. For Earl's Court plans, TfL turned down the FOI on cost grounds but offered a visit to its drawing archive outside FOI ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/drawings_for_earls_court_station)). The other legitimate channels are research visits to the TfL Corporate Archives, which hold 163,000+ physical items and ~26 TB of digital records ([TfL Archives](https://tfl-2.access.preservica.com/about-us/)), London Transport Museum's historic plans and sections, such as a 1948 Farringdon plan ([LTM](https://www.ltmuseum.co.uk/collections/collections-online/drawings/item/1998-10645)), and NDA-bound research or developer engagement. UCL's 2017 work on Crossrail's federated model is precedent for that last route ([ISPRS Annals](https://isprs-annals.copernicus.org/articles/IV-4-W5/49/2017/)). Sydney is the contrast case: Transport for NSW publishes station CAD drawings as open data ([TfNSW](https://opendata.transport.nsw.gov.au/data/dataset/station-cad-drawings-s-v)), and TfL has no equivalent.

Public scans don't fill the gap either. No public point cloud or mesh of a working station interior turned up. The closest items are a CC-BY photogrammetry scan of the disused Crouch End surface station, at 1.9M triangles ([CG Channel](https://www.cgchannel.com/?p=137203)), and CC-BY scans of Tube vents and entrances on Sketchfab ([Sketchfab API](https://api.sketchfab.com/v3/search?type=models&q=tube%20station%20london&count=24)). Cambridge's CC-BY LiDAR datasets cover streets and bridges, not the Underground ([Apollo](https://www.repository.cam.ac.uk/items/b740672a-7d0e-4fb5-bef1-e910310f1b9e)). Google Street View launched inside 18 stations in December 2024 with 18 more due ([TfL](https://tfl.gov.uk/info-for/media/press-releases/2024/december/google-street-view-launches-at-london-s-busiest-stations-making-journeys-easier)). That is imagery under Google's terms, not reusable geometry.

## The open inputs are 2.5D plans, ordinal levels and FOI numbers

**OSM indoor data** is the most valuable open source and the least verified. TfL commissioned MENTZ to map the public areas of "roughly 30 major stations", and named 16 as done: North Greenwich, Aldgate East, Tottenham Court Road, Canning Town, Elephant & Castle, Canada Water, Farringdon, Holborn, Belsize Park, Bank, Arsenal, Notting Hill Gate, Euston Square, Great Portland Street, Hammersmith and Kilburn ([TfL Tech Forum](https://techforum.tfl.gov.uk/t/tfl-osm-station-mapping-project/502)). The models are collected under relation 15700150. The wiki itself warns they are "not consistently tagged or discoverable" ([OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/London_public_transport_tagging_scheme/Underground_navigation_and_access)). Tagging follows the TfL Barrier-Free scheme: `level=`, `indoor=yes`, `highway=steps|elevator`, `conveying=` for escalators, and `step_count=` ([OSM wiki](https://wiki.openstreetmap.org/wiki/London_public_transport_tagging_scheme/TfL_Barrier-Free)). **Unverified:** the research sandbox blocked every Overpass mirror, so nobody counted how many stations actually have level-tagged indoor data today, or how complete each is. Running `[out:json];relation(15700150);rel(r);(._;>>;);out body;` on overpass-turbo is the first thing to do, and it takes five minutes. Expect 2.5D output: polygons per integer level, with no true elevations. OSM2World can extrude indoor tagging to glTF, and has supported rooms, stairs, elevators and level heights since GSoC 2020 ([OSM diary](https://www.openstreetmap.org/user/DWeaver/diary/394059); [FOSDEM 2026](https://fosdem.org/2026/schedule/event/osm2world_3d_rendering_openstreetmap_data)).

**Vertical data** comes from one FOI. In 2011 TfL released "Station depths.xlsx" (48 KB), which gives per-line platform levels relative to sea level ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/depth_of_tube_lines)). MichalPaszkiewicz/tubedepths packages equivalent data as JSON. It has **280 stations and 321 station-line records**, was updated in August 2024 to add the Elizabeth line, and has no licence file ([GitHub](https://github.com/MichalPaszkiewicz/tubedepths)). Its raw CSV adds +100 to every value, which matches TfL's Adjusted Tunnel Datum (ATD = OD + 100 m). Tottenham Court Road's Elizabeth line axonometric labels the platform level "+101.770" ([TfL FOI](https://foi.tfl.gov.uk/FOI-1497-2425/Tottenham%20Court%20Road%203.pdf)), which supports this reading, but the datum mapping is inferred, not documented. Two ambiguities set your vertical error floor. The first is whether values refer to rail or platform surface, worth about 1 m. The second is that independent sources disagree on the deepest station: Hampstead is 58 m, 58.5 m or 58.9 m below ground depending on who you read ([CityMonitor](https://citymonitor.ai/transport/which-london-s-deepest-tube-station-2938); [Tube Alerter](https://tubenotifications.co.uk/insights/how-deep-is-the-tube)). A newer "FOI-0493-2223" CSV cited by Tube Alerter could not be verified. The newer axonometrics give a second, independent check. They state depths below street, such as Embankment's 5.5 / 16.2 / 21.0 m for District, Northern and Bakerloo ([TfL FOI](https://foi.tfl.gov.uk/FOI-1497-2425/Embankment%202.pdf)), and Tottenham Court Road's 26.2 m Central and 29.8 m Northern ([TfL FOI](https://foi.tfl.gov.uk/FOI-1497-2425/Tottenham%20Court%20Road%201.pdf)).

**Topology** is well served. TfL's step-free station topology specification (v1.0, July 2021) publishes GTFS stops, levels and pathways, with level indices, lat/lon of physical points, and boarding gaps in mm ([TfL PDF](https://tfl.gov.uk/cdn/static/cms/documents/step-free-access-and-toilet-data-guide.pdf)). A Valencia re-packaging counts **4,083 stops** with lift, ramp and escalator links ([uv.es](https://www.uv.es/dataenhance/datasets/2526020003.html)). The Unified API adds counts per station, for example 5 lifts, 15 escalators and 29 gates for Bank. Entrance coordinates can simply copy the parent station point, so they are unreliable ([TfL API](https://api.tfl.gov.uk/StopPoint/940GZZLUBNK); [Tech Forum](https://techforum.tfl.gov.uk/t/a-couple-of-inconsistencies-in-tube-station-entrance-data/1257)). For horizontal scale, a 2009 FOI produced platform lengths in metres for every LU platform ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/platform_lengths_of_tfl_rail_ser)).

**The axonometrics** are the best visual reference and the worst data source. TfL released them on 6 July 2015 to Georg Vehres as 8 per-line PDFs, with s38(1)(b) and s40(2) redactions ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/maps_of_public_corridors_on_larg)). IanVisits republished about 120 images across five alphabetical pages ([IanVisits](https://www.ianvisits.co.uk/blog/2015/07/12/3d-maps-of-every-underground-station-hijklm/)). The published figures conflict: "120 stations" against "124 maps", which probably reflects multi-sheet stations such as Waterloo's three sheets. They exist only for "Section 12" sub-surface stations ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/axonometric_drawings_of_every_st)), so **roughly 150 surface stations have none**. Baker Street and Mornington Crescent were missing from the 2015 set. The diagrams are "not to scale" and "not updated", so Bank, TCR, Bond Street, Paddington, Battersea and the other rebuilt stations are stale in that set. Targeted FOIs in 2023–24 did produce updated sheets for seven stations plus Charing Cross Bakerloo ([WhatDoTheyKnow](https://www.whatdotheyknow.com/request/axonometric_of_bakerloo_line_ex)). They remain TfL copyright: an FOI release grants access, not a licence to re-use. Use them for adjacency and stacking order, and transcribe their depth labels as facts. Never measure off the linework.

The only open whole-station meshes are in **andrewgodwin/stations**. It covers Bank (87 objects, z from −40 to −2), King's Cross St Pancras (59), Oxford Circus (46), Victoria, Walthamstow Central, Old Street and Seven Sisters, plus 4 DLR, Overground and cable-car stations. The models are Blender 2.62 files and legacy three.js JSON, with no commits since July 2012 ([GitHub](https://github.com/andrewgodwin/stations)). The author's own notes say Bank was "done on foot… angles and positioning may be slightly wrong" and Walthamstow "entirely from memory". They are useful as a topology sanity check and as a Blender scaffold you can correct. The NC-SA licence rules out commercial use.

## Games and marketplaces sell likeness, not measurement

Dovetail's **Train Sim World Bakerloo line** is the most complete licensed recreation. It models **25 stations, 15 of them deep-level**, under a TfL trademark licence, and is now on TSW7 ([Epic Games Store](https://store.epicgames.com/p/train-sim-world-6--bakerloo-line); [Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/2970120/Train_Sim_World_5_Bakerloo_Line_Route_AddOn)). Players can walk the platforms ([TheSixthAxis](https://www.thesixthaxis.com/2020/08/25/train-sim-world-2-review)). **Unverified:** no source confirms modelled ticket halls or escalator shafts. **World of Subways 3** advertised "free movement… at the stations" across 35 Circle line stations, but was delisted on 3 March 2025 when its licence expired ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/World_of_Subways)). Just Trains' Metropolitan line has 34 stations seen from the cab ([Steam](https://store.steampowered.com/app/1230277)). Fan builds such as Pimlico in Minecraft, Aldwych in Garry's Mod and The London Project's Canary Wharf, Waterloo and Charing Cross are dormant or unreleased, and block-scale at best ([Planet Minecraft](https://www.planetminecraft.com/server/the-london-project/)). Extracting geometry from licensed games almost certainly breaches the EULA. Dovetail's own player EULA could not be retrieved, so that rests on standard industry terms. It would also drag in TfL's IP, since TfL explicitly licenses "livery and environments – station architecture and visual identity" ([TfL IP guidance](https://content.tfl.gov.uk/tfl-ip-guidance.pdf)).

Marketplaces add little. The one "real station" listing on CGTrader is a $19 King's Cross Rhino model sold under an editorial licence, and it is built from the 2012 aeracode models, so it inherits their memory-based inaccuracy ([CGTrader](https://www.cgtrader.com/3d-models/architectural/engineering/kings-cross-st-pancras-underground-station-in-london)). TurboSquid's "London metro stations" packs ($49–79, editorial-only) name King's Cross once; the rest look generic ([TurboSquid](https://www.turbosquid.com/3d-models/london-metro-stations-vii-2309781)). A 1.3M-face Baker Street interior on Sketchfab is view-only and carries no licence ([Sketchfab](https://api.sketchfab.com/v3/models/f0c24a9b170a45639444d64a32eb0f2f)). The Station Master app has 3D diagrams of "all 270" stations, but they are closed, illustrative and of undisclosed method ([Station Master](https://stationmasterapp.com/3dmaps.html)). Treat all of these as reference imagery, not inputs. SketchUp 3D Warehouse and Fab could not be searched, so a stray accurate model there cannot be ruled out.

## A scaled whole-station model takes a five-layer fusion

Build each layer from the source that is actually authoritative for it, and never let a weak source set a dimension that a strong one can.

**Layer 1, graph.** Pull the station's GTFS pathways and levels, or the Stop Structure API, to fix which spaces exist, which level index each sits on, and which connectors (lift, escalator, stairs) join them. Cross-check counts against the Unified API's `additionalProperties`. This gives you a correct skeleton before any geometry.

**Layer 2, plan.** Where MENTZ mapped the station, import the OSM indoor polygons per level. Elsewhere, trace platform alignments from OSM's `railway=platform` ways and fix their lengths to the FOI platform-length spreadsheet. CartoMetro is an excellent scaled guide to track and platform positions, but it is "reproduction prohibited" ([CartoMetro](https://cartometro.com/documents/CartoMetroLondon.v4.6.pdf)), so consult it rather than trace it.

**Layer 3, z.** Assign each line's platform the elevation from the depth spreadsheet or tubedepths, converted consistently to OD or ATD. Check it against any axonometric "m below street" label, and take ground level at each entrance from Environment Agency LiDAR DTM. Where the two depth sources disagree by more than about 1 m, investigate before averaging.

**Layer 4, connectors.** Derive escalator geometry from the level difference. My inference, based on standard 30° escalators, is horizontal run ≈ 1.73 × rise and incline length ≈ 2 × rise. Angel's published 27 m rise and 61 m length (incline plus landings) is consistent with this ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Angel_tube_station)). Shafts and lifts then follow from where the graph says they land. Take passage routing and stacking order from the axonometric, or from aeracode where one exists. Treat passage widths and lengths as guesses until measured. Standard tube tunnel diameters were not sourced in this research, so verify them from engineering references before you hard-code them.

**Layer 5, validate.** Walk the public areas. Time the passages, count steps, and photograph. Optionally capture concourses with phone LiDAR, as a Toronto mapper did for a subway concourse before tracing it into OSM ([wilkinson.graphics](https://wilkinson.graphics/blog/2025-10-25-3d-mapping/)). TfL's policy on scanning equipment in stations was not verified, and commercial filming needs permission ([TfL filming guide](https://tfl.gov.uk/info-for/media/filming-and-photography/guide-to-filming-or-taking-photos-on-the-tfl-network)). Feeding your corrections back into OSM improves the commons and keeps you inside ODbL.

My estimates of the resulting accuracy, which nobody has benchmarked against survey data, are as follows. Platforms: ±5–20 m horizontally where the OSM data is TfL-sourced, worse elsewhere. Levels: ±1–2 m vertically. Passages: topologically correct but ±30% in length unless scanned. That is roughly three orders of magnitude worse than the ±10 mm of a professional scan-to-BIM ([HitechDigital](https://hitechdigital.com/case-studies/bim/point-cloud-conversion-for-underground-railway-tunnel-uk)). It is fine for visualisation, wayfinding or a game, and useless for engineering. On cost, a deep interchange like Bank plausibly takes tens of hours in Blender; a two-level Victoria line station takes a few. A scripted pipeline (OSM → OSM2World or custom extrusion → z-patch from depth JSON → glTF) amortises well across the ~16 MENTZ stations and degrades sharply outside them. On licensing: OSM-derived databases carry ODbL share-alike. The numeric FOI facts are on safer ground than traced axonometric linework. Stripping roundels and Johnston type from the output avoids TfL's trademark claims.

## Conclusion

The bottleneck is legal and security policy, not data scarcity. London has arguably some of the best-scanned metro stations in the world, and TfL's consistent s24/s38 stance means none of it will reach the public through FOI. The practical consequence is that an open model is a reconstruction problem, and the hard axis is vertical. Plan geometry is partly solved by OSM. The depth figures, however, come from a 2011 spreadsheet with an undocumented datum and rail-versus-platform ambiguity, and they predate the Elizabeth line, the Northern line extension and the Bank upgrade. A fresh FOI for current platform and concourse levels in ATD, phrased as numbers rather than drawings, is the single highest-leverage request anyone could make. The precedents suggest TfL releases numeric tables and schematic material far more readily than drawings.

Two unverified items decide how much work you face, and both are cheap to resolve. First, an Overpass count of relation 15700150 shows whether the "~30 stations" import was completed and how many stations you can bootstrap automatically. Second, a look at TSW Bakerloo gameplay shows whether any licensed product models a station from street to platform, which matters as a visual reference even though its geometry cannot be extracted. If you have a specific station in mind, check it against the 16 MENTZ stations first. Inside that set, a credible scaled model is a weekend-scale scripting project. Outside it, you are hand-modelling from schematics and the platform-length spreadsheet.
